\heading{What could be misaligned?}
\begin{points}
\item \textbf{Start delay:} storing sound in a computer buffer adds delay. As an example, one buffer of 256 samples at 48,000 samples per second lasts \textbf{5.3 milliseconds}; two last 10.7 milliseconds. These are calculations, not measurements of this interface.
\item \textbf{Sound travel:} the speaker and converters add delay. Air adds about \textbf{3 milliseconds per metre} from speaker to ear.
\item \textbf{Loudness accuracy:} unknown until each tone is checked at the ear.
\item \textbf{Changing delay between trials:} unknown until we test it. Preload the sound sequence and use wired connections.
\item \textbf{Drift:} the sound and brain recorders may run at slightly different speeds. Our mismatch is unknown. For scale only, a hypothetical 0.01\% speed mismatch would cause \textbf{0.36 seconds of error in one hour} without correction.
\end{points}
\heading{How we check the timing}
\begin{points}
\item Send a timing reference beside each tone and record that reference with the brain signals.
\item Use the microphone to measure the gap between the reference and sound arriving at the ear.
\item Recording the microphone only on the sound interface leaves two timelines to align. A sound output is not automatically a compatible digital trigger; check the recorder input.
\end{points}

\heading{How precise is it?}
\begin{points}
\item \textbf{Pitch range:} the paper tested \textbf{1.4--32 kilohertz}. It used about 80 decibels at the ear, but gives no measured sound-level uncertainty.
\item \textbf{Reported neural-processing delay: 56 milliseconds.} This covers neural measurement, filtering and collecting data for decoding. It excludes sensory processing and computer calculation time. A separate one-session analysis reports \textbf{11 milliseconds}.
\item \textbf{Sound-to-brain recording alignment:} remaining timing error and long-session drift are not quantified. The time reference specifies below one microsecond plus network variation, not sound-arrival accuracy.
\item \textbf{Drift:} the independent sound-device clock specification corresponds to \textbf{180 milliseconds per hour versus ideal time}. This is not the paper's drift with satellite timing connected. Drift against our recorder remains unknown.
\end{points}

\heading{How precise is it?}
\begin{points}
\item \textbf{Pitch range:} the speaker is specified for \textbf{4--110 kilohertz}. It misses the paper's lowest 1.4--4 kilohertz tones. The board's flat output extends to 92.1 kilohertz at 204,800 samples per second.
\item \textbf{Start delay:} the sound board's output filter adds \textbf{0.15625 milliseconds} at its highest sample rate. Speaker and air delays are additional. The slides give no measured alignment with brain recording.
\item \textbf{Drift:} the sound board's internal-clock specification corresponds to \textbf{72 milliseconds per hour versus ideal time} with current calibration; aging adds uncertainty. Clock settings and our recorder determine actual relative drift; that remains unknown.
\item \textbf{Loudness at the ear:} accuracy is unmeasured. The speaker's uncorrected response variation is $\pm$11 decibels; that is not calibrated accuracy.
\end{points}
