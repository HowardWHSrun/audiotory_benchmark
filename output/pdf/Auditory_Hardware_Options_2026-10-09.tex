\heading{What precision can we actually claim?}
\textbf{Tone frequency.} Cheap audio hardware can generate the chosen tones, but I found no guaranteed clock error in Hz for the proposed interface. NI specifies a 50 ppm clock: that contributes about \textbf{\(\pm\)0.5 Hz at 10 kHz or \(\pm\)1.6 Hz at 32 kHz}, provided we generate the waveform using the actual sample rate returned by the driver. These are clock calculations, not measured sound accuracy. \buy{https://download.ni.com/support/manuals/374650c.pdf}{NI specification}; \buy{https://knowledge.ni.com/KnowledgeArticleDetails?id=kA00Z000000P83OSAS\&l=en-US}{actual sample rate}.

\textbf{Sound level at the ear.} The \buy{https://www.reedinstruments.com/pdfs/cache/www.reedinstruments.com/r8090/datasheet/r8090-datasheet.pdf}{reference calibrator} is specified at \textbf{\(\pm\)0.5 dB at 1 kHz}. It does not guarantee every tone at the ear is within \(\pm\)0.5 dB. We still use the microphone's correction file and measure each tone at the chosen position. Whole-system SPL accuracy is unmeasured for every option.

\textbf{Sound-to-brain timing.} At 192,000 samples/s, waveform samples are \textbf{5.2 microseconds apart}; a proposed 100,000-sample/s Arduino design has a 10-microsecond grid. Neither is a claim of neural alignment accuracy. Record physical markers and check sound arrival/clock drift with our recorder. Faster digital triggers do not remove speaker or acoustic delay.

\heading{What SONIC tells us}
\buy{https://doi.org/10.1101/2025.09.30.679683}{SONIC v1, Methods 6.1--6.2, p.11}: USB-6361 $\rightarrow$ SA1 $\rightarrow$ MF1; 1.4--32 kHz initial tones, 10 ms benchmark tones, 2 ms ramps, nominal 192,000 samples/s and about 80 dB at the ears. It used PTP/10 MHz synchronization and recorded output/trial references. It does \textbf{not} report measured \(\pm\)Hz, \(\pm\)dB or sound-to-neural jitter. Its sheep were awake; our sedated-sheep responses remain unverified. The large rack in the slides is not needed for this USB approach.

\heading{Where used equipment helps}
The \money{325} preamp saves \money{620} against new. I also found a \buy{https://www.ebay.com/itm/304731172673}{USB-6361 at \money{1,395} used}, but it is a mass-terminated model without supply/accessories: it needs a different cable/breakout. Its price is not a complete replacement cost. A \buy{https://www.ebay.com/itm/318503133762}{USB-6211 at \money{600} used} is a cheaper engineering candidate, not the paper's device; \buy{https://www.ni.com/docs/en-US/bundle/usb-6211-specs/page/specs.html}{settling and shared-input limits} need checking at 32 kHz. Substituting it would bring the estimate to \textbf{\money{8,271.95}}, if those checks pass. Neither used NI device is counted in the main total. TDT components remain quote-only.

\heading{What our own program would do}
Prepare the tone list and waveforms on the computer, then preload a block. Let the Arduino's timer and buffered output schedule the samples; generate the marker from the same hardware event as each tone, rather than a USB message. Capture microphone/output references and save repeated markers with the neural data. \buy{https://github.com/arduino-libraries/Arduino_AdvancedAnalog/blob/main/docs/api.md}{Arduino's library} supplies buffered analog I/O, but common ADC/DAC timing and the marker need firmware work. FPGA audio needs similar code plus clock/codec setup; it has no demonstrated precision advantage here.

\textbf{My choice:} start with the \money{700} audible setup if those tones answer our question. Build the \money{100}--\money{150} Arduino prototype alongside it if custom control matters. Spend on the full-range speaker/mic when tones above 20 kHz are required. All options need acoustic and recorder timing checks before their final precision can be claimed.
