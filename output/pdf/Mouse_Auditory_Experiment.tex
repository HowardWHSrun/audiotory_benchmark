{\LARGE\bfseries Our sound setup}\par
{\color{muted}For mice, with later sheep use in mind \hfill October 8, 2026}\par

We want to play a tone, record the brain's response with our lab's probes, and save when the tone happened. We will build the sound setup for mice and aim to reuse it for sheep.

\topic{How the parts fit together}
\begin{center}
{\small\fbox{Computer} $\rightarrow$ \fbox{NI sound output} $\rightarrow$ \fbox{Amplifier} $\rightarrow$ \fbox{Speaker} $\rightarrow$ \fbox{Mouse}}
\end{center}
\begin{itemize}
\item The computer prepares the tones. The NI box sends the signal, the amplifier drives the speaker, and the speaker plays the sound.
\item A microphone checks the sound where the animal's ear will be.
\item A timing signal goes to the lab's brain recorder, so we can match each tone to the recorded response.
\end{itemize}

\topic{1. Decide what to play}
\begin{itemize}
\item Choose how high or low each tone is, how long it lasts, how loud it is, and the gap between tones.
\item Prepare and save the full tone sequence before we play it. Keep it with the recordings so we can identify the tone for each trial.
\end{itemize}

\topic{2. Check what reaches the ear}
\begin{itemize}
\item Fix the speaker in place and record its distance and angle.
\item Put the microphone at the ear position. Check every tone's level and waveform, then adjust the output as needed.
\item Keep the calibration and background-sound records with the experiment files.
\end{itemize}

\topic{3. Match the sound to the brain recording}
\begin{itemize}
\item Have the NI box send a timing pulse when each tone starts, and record those pulses with the brain signals. First check that the lab recorder can accept them.
\item The pulse marks the electrical start. Match the microphone recording to those pulses, then check when the sound actually arrives.
\item Check that the sound and brain-recording times stay aligned throughout a recording.
\end{itemize}

{\small\color{muted}We still need to choose the mouse tone settings and whether the mouse is awake or sedated. Rice's sheep plan uses sedation; SONIC studied awake sheep. The appendix shows what SONIC did and what we would change.}

\begin{itemize}

\begin{itemize}
\item We use our own lab's probes. This list covers the sound side; it does not budget for new neural hardware or animal preparation.

{\LARGE\bfseries Appendix: why these choices?}\par

\topic{The speaker and the two parts it needs}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Speaker (MF2-M):} we want one through-air speaker we could use for mice and later sheep. TDT lists a 1--80 kHz range, which includes SONIC's explored 1.4--32 kHz range. We still have to measure our chosen tones at the ear.
\item \textbf{Amplifier (SA1):} the NI box makes the electrical waveform. SA1 provides the power to drive the speaker. TDT supports this combination, and SONIC also used SA1.
\item \textbf{Power unit (ZB1PS):} SA1 needs TDT's zBus power connection. This unit powers and houses it, so it belongs with the amplifier.
\end{itemize}

\begin{itemize}
\item SONIC used 10 ms sine tones with 2 ms fades at each end, in random balanced blocks without gaps. It prepared the waveform before playback.
\item It reported about 80 dB SPL at the sheep-ear distance. We have not chosen our level. Its clock server is not in this shopping list.
\end{itemize}

\topic{What we would change for sheep}
\begin{itemize}
\item Aim to reuse the speaker, amplifier, power unit, NI, computer, and sound recorder.
\item Move the mounts and check every tone again at the sheep-ear position. Redo the calibration and timing checks for that recording system.
\item Check the lowest tone before buying: the kit's microphone starts at 2 kHz, so SONIC's 1.4 kHz tones need another suitable microphone.
\item Choose the tone settings and response windows for sedated sheep. SONIC used awake sheep; the same hardware does not guarantee the same brain responses.
\end{itemize}
